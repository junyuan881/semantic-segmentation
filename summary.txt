I implemented two binary semantic segmentation models, UNet and ResNet34_UNet, for the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset. GPT was only used for the first draft of the code structure; the later debugging, architecture revision, and most implementation details were completed by myself to match the referenced papers.

For ResNet34_UNet, I used ResNet34 as the encoder and a UNet-style decoder. I corrected an inconsistency in Huang et al.’s figure and designed the decoder with bilinear upsampling and two convolution layers per block. Batch Normalization was added in the ResNet encoder, and the input images were resized to 224×224. For UNet, I followed Ronneberger et al.(2015) and implemented the original 23-layer architecture. Since the paper uses 572×572 input and 388×388 output, I resized the supervision region to 388×388 and applied mirroring padding to the input.

For preprocessing, I followed the provided split files and converted trimaps into binary masks. During EDA, I found problematic masks such as keeshond_7, Abyssinian_34, and Egyptian_Mau_129. I removed training samples whose foreground ratio was below 5%. I also applied random horizontal flipping, rotation, brightness, contrast, and saturation adjustment, followed by normalization.

For training, I used Adam with weight decay 1e-4. The initial learning rate was 1e-3, and a scheduler halved it if validation Dice did not improve for three epochs, down to 1e-6 for UNet and 5e-7 for ResNet34_UNet. The loss was 0.3*BCEWithLogitsLoss+0.7*DiceLoss, and I used Gaussian-based initialization for UNet.

My key observations are that filtering nearly empty masks improved performance, normalization was especially important for UNet, and hybrid loss worked better than cross-entropy alone. Data augmentation improved generalization, but excessive augmentation reduced stability. Overall, ResNet34_UNet converged faster, trained more stably, and achieved better segmentation performance than UNet, while UNet remained a simpler baseline.
